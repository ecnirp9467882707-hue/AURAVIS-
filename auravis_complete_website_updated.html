01 / The concept

What is AURAVIS?

A student research concept combining embedded vision, image processing and exploration of future retinal-interface technologies.

🎯

Research objective

Capture environmental images and convert them into simplified visual information for a simulated retinal display.

👁️

The medical challenge

Different causes of blindness affect different parts of the visual system. A technology must account for the condition of retinal cells, optic nerve and brain pathways.

💡

Our approach

Combine a compact camera, computer vision, image enhancement and a conceptual model of a future retinal interface.

Problem statement

Vision loss can arise from conditions affecting the cornea, lens, retina, optic nerve or brain. AURAVIS investigates a possible technology pathway for visual information capture and processing, without claiming to cure blindness.

Project objectives

  1. Capture live video using ESP32-CAM.
  2. Transmit the video stream to a computer over Wi-Fi.
  3. Apply grayscale conversion, contrast enhancement and edge detection.
  4. Represent processed information on a screen or LED matrix.
  5. Study eye anatomy and the visual pathway.
  6. Understand the safety and clinical validation required for a medical device.
02 / System workflow

How the concept works

The current demonstration covers image capture and digital processing. Retinal delivery is a proposed research direction, not a working feature.

Visual capture

ESP32-CAM records the surrounding scene.

Transmission

Video is sent over Wi-Fi to a computer.

Image processing

Software enhances contrast, converts to grayscale and detects edges.

Information encoding

Image features are represented as a simplified visual pattern.

Retinal simulation

A screen or LED matrix represents proposed output; it does not stimulate living retinal cells.

System architecture

Environment → ESP32-CAM → Wi-Fi → Computer / OpenCV → Visual pattern → Screen simulation
03 / Ophthalmology fundamentals

Human eye anatomy

The eye is an optical and biological system. The retina is not simply a screen: it is specialised neural tissue that begins processing visual information.

StructureMedical functionRelevance to AURAVIS
CorneaTransparent front surface; provides much of the eye's focusing power.Any proposed optical path must account for refraction and safety.
Iris and pupilIris changes pupil size to regulate incoming light.Shows that the eye naturally regulates light entry.
Crystalline lensFine-tunes focus so an image forms on the retina.A model lens can demonstrate focusing; it is not an implant.
RetinaLight-sensitive neural tissue lining the back of the eye.Conceptual target region; our model uses a simulated retina.
RodsPhotoreceptors important for dim-light and peripheral vision.Biological light sensors, not a simple projection screen.
ConesPhotoreceptors important for colour and fine detail, especially in brighter light.Show why normal vision needs specialised living cells.
Retinal ganglion cellsCollect processed retinal information; their axons form the optic nerve.Some retinal prosthesis approaches interact with surviving neurons.
Optic nerveCarries visual information from the retina toward the brain.A functioning downstream pathway is important.
Visual cortexBrain region involved in processing and interpreting vision.Vision is not produced by the eye alone.

How natural vision occurs

  1. Light reflected from an object enters through the cornea.
  2. The pupil regulates incoming light.
  3. The lens helps focus the image onto the retina.
  4. Rods and cones respond to light and initiate neural signalling.
  5. Retinal circuits process the signals.
  6. Signals travel through the optic nerve and visual pathways.
  7. The brain interprets the information as visual perception.
04 / Medical science

Why light projection alone cannot restore vision

A projected optical image is not equivalent to repairing photoreceptors or creating a useful neural signal.

In normal sight, photoreceptors respond to light and initiate biological signalling. If photoreceptors or other parts of the visual pathway are damaged, projecting an image onto the retina may not produce useful vision. The retina contains multiple cell types and neural circuits, and the brain must interpret the resulting signals.

Research approachBasic principleImportant distinction
Optical projectionForms an image using light and optical components.Needs functioning light-sensitive biological cells to convert light into neural signals.
Retinal prosthesisAn implanted electrode array electrically stimulates selected retinal neurons.Electrical neural interface, not simply a laser shining an image onto the retina.
OptogeneticsResearch approach using genetic methods to make selected cells respond to light.Specialised biomedical research; not part of this student prototype.
Cortical visual prosthesisResearch devices aim to stimulate visual regions of the brain.Targets the brain rather than projecting an image onto the retina.
Who could a future technology help? It depends on the cause and location of vision loss, the condition of remaining retinal cells, the optic nerve and the brain's visual pathways. A device cannot be described as suitable for all blind people. Patient suitability must be assessed by qualified medical specialists.
05 / Optical physics

Laser science: Wavelength, Frequency and Power

Laser parameters are engineering and safety specifications. They are not values that can be guessed from the desired colour of a beam.

〰️

Wavelength (λ)

Distance between successive wave peaks, commonly measured in nanometres (nm).

⏱️

Frequency (f)

Number of wave cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz).

⚡

Optical power

Rate at which optical energy is emitted, commonly expressed in watts or milliwatts.

c = f × λ

In a vacuum: c is the speed of light, f is frequency and λ is wavelength. Wavelength and frequency are inversely related for electromagnetic radiation in a given medium.

Parameters a qualified team would need to evaluate

ParameterMeaningAURAVIS project status
Wavelength (nm)Optical wavelength emitted by the source.Not selected; no ocular laser is installed.
Optical frequency (Hz)Wave cycles per second, related to wavelength by c = f × λ.Not specified; no source is selected.
Output power (W or mW)Rate of optical energy emission.Not specified; no eye-directed source is used.
Pulse durationDuration of each pulse from a pulsed source.Not applicable to current model.
Repetition rate (Hz)Number of pulses emitted per second.Not applicable to current model.
Beam diameter and divergenceDescribe beam size and how it spreads.Not specified; requires complete optical design.
Exposure durationHow long tissue is exposed to optical energy.No human or animal exposure.
Maximum permissible exposure (MPE)Exposure limit determined using applicable standards and conditions.Must be assessed by qualified laser-safety professionals for any future device.
Why no laser power or frequency setting? There is no universal safe laser setting for projecting images into a human eye. A safety assessment needs the complete source and exposure information, optical geometry, beam characteristics, exposure time, operating mode and applicable standards. An incorrect value could cause permanent eye injury. AURAWIS does not propose or test an eye-directed laser setting.

Image-processing concepts

  • Grayscale: represents an image using intensity rather than full colour.
  • Contrast enhancement: increases differences between darker and brighter regions.
  • Edge detection: identifies boundaries and strong intensity changes.
  • Thresholding: separates pixels into groups using an intensity threshold.
  • Object detection: AI task that identifies and locates objects such as people, chairs or vehicles.

08 / Safety and ethics

Limitations, safety and medical validation

A credible biomedical project explains what it can do, what it cannot do and what evidence would be required next.

Laser safety: Never direct a laser or intense light into a human or animal eye. Do not use a laser pointer to demonstrate retinal projection. Use a monitor, LED matrix or printed pattern for the simulation. A dim-looking or invisible beam is not automatically safe.
  1. Different causes of blindness: damage may occur in different parts of the visual pathway.
  2. Biological response: light projection cannot replace photoreceptors or guarantee a neural response.
  3. Neural coding: natural vision involves complex patterns of activity across retinal cells.
  4. Resolution: a camera image contains more information than a simple simulated display can reproduce.
  5. Optical safety: any eye-directed source requires strict evaluation to prevent tissue damage.
  6. Implant safety: an implant requires biocompatibility, sterilisation and mechanical reliability.
  7. Power and heat: wearable or implantable electronics must manage battery life, temperature and electrical safety.
  8. Clinical evidence: effectiveness and patient suitability must be established through medical research.

Before any medical use, what would be needed?

  • Consultation with ophthalmologists, retinal specialists and biomedical engineers.
  • Optical modelling and independent laser-safety assessment, if a laser were ever considered.
  • Electrical, thermal and biocompatibility testing.
  • Bench testing with non-living models and suitable laboratory equipment.
  • Ethics and regulatory review, followed by properly authorised clinical studies.
